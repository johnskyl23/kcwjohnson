Why make robots smaller?

Miniaturization to the near-gram size enables qualitatively different ways of deploying robotic systems.

Near-gram robots can enter narrow gaps, cavities, dense vegetation, machinery, damaged structures, and other environments that are difficult or impossible for conventional robots or people to access. That creates opportunities in industrial inspection, environmental monitoring, disaster response, precision agriculture, and distributed sensing.

Rather than relying on a few highly capable and expensive robots, sufficiently inexpensive microrobots could be deployed as large fleets that gather measurements at many locations simultaneously — where robustness emerges from the number and distribution of devices rather than from adding redundant hardware to each one.

But the very scaling that enables this removes many of the resources traditionally used to create autonomy. At the near-gram scale, each additional milligram, cubic millimeter, and milliwatt represents a significant fraction of the total system budget.

The near-gram autonomy gap

Full autonomy has to fit inside the same gram.

Mobility actuation, onboard sensing, onboard computing, a wireless link, and onboard power. Many impressive microrobots achieve one of these by moving another outside the robot — a tether, a motion-capture rig, an offboard controller, a magnetic field generator.

So the question stops being how do we miniaturize each component, and becomes: where should each function required for autonomous behavior actually live?

The framework

Cyber-Mechanical
Intelligence

A co-design paradigm that treats the location of intelligence within the robot as a design variable.

CMI as a system-level design framework. Four interacting pillars provide complementary means of realizing autonomous functions; the resulting allocation depends on the task, environment, and available system resources.

In this work, “intelligence” is not synonymous with artificial intelligence or computation. Intelligence refers more broadly to a system’s capacity to produce appropriate autonomous behavior in response to its task and environment. That behavior may arise from computation — but it may also be produced through mechanical dynamics, morphology, passive stability, stored mechanical state, specialized electronics, or combinations of these domains.

Functions are not assigned to mechanisms, electronics, or computation by convention, but according to where they can be implemented with the lowest system-level resource cost while preserving the behavior the task requires.

The goal is not to replace computation with physical mechanisms wherever possible. It is to jointly design the physical, electronic, and computational system so that each domain performs the functions for which it is best suited.

— Cyber-Mechanical Intelligence: Creating Near-Gram Robots That Can Move Autonomously, Ph.D. dissertation, University of Washington, 2026

PILLAR 01

Dynamic mechanisms

Morphology, passive dynamics, compliance, material properties, and mechanical state treated as resources that can perform functions conventionally assigned to sensing, computation, control, or actuation.

PILLAR 02

Efficient sensing & actuation

The minimum sensing and actuation necessary to accomplish the task reliably — task-specific sensors, event-driven sampling, mechanically amplified actuation, low-degree-of-freedom control.

PILLAR 03

Resource-aware embedded systems

Energy harvesting, power management, intermittent computing, duty cycling, and energy-aware control — so energy availability becomes part of the operating strategy rather than a constraint imposed on a fixed architecture.

PILLAR 04

On-device intelligence

State estimation, model-based control, planning, learned perception, and resource-constrained neural inference — used where adaptability and information processing provide the greatest value per unit of system cost.

The systems

Four robots,
four allocations.

Each platform addresses a different resource bottleneck — and arrives at a different answer to the same question.

01

Intelligent morphology

Origami Microflier

Shape can change lateral displacement. So let the shape hold the decision.

Wind-dispersed sensor nodes can exploit environmental airflow instead of carrying propulsion — but conventional passive microfliers have little control over where they land. The Origami Microflier uses a reconfigurable mechanical structure to change how the surrounding airflow acts on the device.

The 414 mg system is built around a bistable leaf-out origami structure with two mechanically stable configurations. Unfolded, it tumbles as it falls and is more strongly influenced by lateral wind. After transitioning to its folded state, the altered geometry produces a more symmetric aerodynamic response and a stable downward descent. Because the origami is bistable, each configuration is maintained mechanically — no continuous power or control is required to preserve it.

Harvested solar energy is accumulated and discharged in a single short actuation event, triggered by programmed timing, an onboard pressure-based altitude estimate, or a wireless command. A brief discharge of electrical energy therefore produces a persistent change in behavior.

02

Intelligent energy allocation

MilliMobile

Stop asking the robot to move continuously. Ask it to move when it can afford to.

Ambient solar and RF sources can provide enough energy over time to support near-gram robotic operation, but their instantaneous power is far below what is needed to continuously drive even small motors. MilliMobile separates the rate at which energy is harvested from the rate at which it is consumed: ambient energy is accumulated in lightweight capacitors and periodically discharged to produce discrete episodes of motion.

The result is a centimeter-square, battery-free robot that moves on as little as 50 µW of harvested power, including in practical indoor lighting. It uses four onboard photodiodes to estimate the direction of incident light and steer toward brighter regions — simultaneously performing its task and seeking environments that supply more energy.

Temperature and humidity sensing and long-range Bluetooth complete the platform: these robots can communicate roughly fifty thousand times their own body length.

03

Intelligence as a scheduled resource

MilliMobile Vision

Run the cheap thing often. Run the expensive thing only when the answer changes what you do.

MilliMobile Vision pushes the same principle into perception. Camera acquisition, image processing, learned inference, and communication all compete for the same limited computing resources, so the system treats perception and computation as schedulable activities — just as the original MilliMobile treats locomotion.

The architecture co-designs sensing, inference, and actuation around two operating modes: a low-rate discovery mode that searches for objects of interest, and a higher-rate tracking mode that prioritizes closed-loop following once a target is detected. Frequent, inexpensive geometric localization drives steering; a more expensive learned classifier is invoked only when semantic identity becomes relevant to the task.

The camera is reused for both target tracking and coarse obstacle detection rather than adding a dedicated range sensor — onboard perception that lets the robot make increasingly sophisticated local decisions without depending on external computation.

04

Intelligent passive flight dynamics

Coin-Copter

A flybar can do the job of an attitude controller — and it never runs out of battery.

Multi-rotor drones maintain attitude by continuously sensing orientation and modulating multiple actuators to regulate roll, pitch, yaw, and position. At the near-gram scale, each additional actuator, sensor, drive circuit, and feedback loop consumes a significant fraction of the available mass, power, and computation.

Coin-Copter instead uses a single high-lift main rotor together with a passive flybar-rotor mechanism — microfabricated from a flat composite laminate, using flexure joints to reproduce the motions of the articulated flybars used in larger helicopters. When the vehicle rotates in roll or pitch, the coupled dynamics of the flybar and teetering rotor tilt the rotor disk relative to the body; the resulting change in thrust direction produces a restoring torque. Stabilization emerges from the rotor mechanics rather than from attitude estimation and active correction.

A small tail rotor retains active control of the remaining yaw axis. A Coin-Copter without the flybar was unable to maintain sustained upright flight, whereas the flybar-equipped system held a stabilized attitude, recovered after a collision, and continued ascending to the limit of the test arena.

FLEABOT

One robot, two gaits.

FLEABOT takes its mobility from insects, and its multimodal locomotion from the same capacitor that powers it. Rather than adding a second drivetrain, the robot reads its own charge curve: the low end of the curve supports walking, and the high end supports a jump.

The jump itself is amplified mechanically. A millimetre-scale Kresling origami spring stores the electromagnetic actuator’s work and releases it in one stroke, so a small actuator produces a hop many times the robot’s body height — the actuation cost is paid once, by the structure, rather than continuously by the motor.

Where this goes

From individual robots to fleets.

Many of the applications motivating near-gram robots become more compelling when the robots operate as fleets rather than as isolated devices. A fleet of mobile sensing nodes could disperse over a large area, reposition to sample spatial gradients, move toward events of interest, and keep providing useful measurements even if some individual robots fail.

The hard part is that coordination must respect the same constraints that shape each robot individually. Battery-free robots may not be awake or powered at the same time, so swarm algorithms have to tolerate intermittent connectivity and heterogeneous energy states rather than assume a continuously available link.

Lessons

Design principles.

What three very different robots taught me about where to put the intelligence.

01

Intelligence is a system property, not a processor property

The relevant question is not how much computation a robot performs, but how effectively the complete cyber-mechanical system transforms limited resources into autonomous behavior.

02

Treat the location of intelligence as a design variable

Identify the functions the robot must perform — maintaining stability, retaining state, estimating the environment, selecting an action — then decide where each can be implemented most efficiently.

03

Minimize active sensing and control when the task permits

Replace continuous control with passive dynamics only when the physical system can perform the function reliably enough. Sense what changes the robot’s action — measurements that don’t are not worth their resource cost.

04

Actuation is often the dominant resource constraint

First reduce how often, how long, and across how many degrees of freedom actuation is required — before increasing actuator capability or energy storage.

05

Use computation where it has the highest system value

CMI does not assume mechanics are preferable to computation. The goal is not minimum computation; it is computation used where adaptability and information processing most improve behavior per unit of system cost.

06

Energy availability shapes behavior

Energy is part of the behavioral state of the robot. When resources are intermittent, the controller should determine not only what action to take, but whether the system can currently afford to sense, compute, communicate, or move.

About

Kyle Johnson

I’m a Ph.D. candidate in Computer Science & Engineering at the University of Washington, advised by Vikram Iyer and Sawyer Fuller, and co-founder of the nonprofit AVELA.

My research explores how combinations of low-power actuation and control mechanisms can create autonomous microrobots optimized for resource-constrained applications. I envision these microrobots operating intelligently in large swarms, alongside larger systems, to transform disaster response, industrial inventory and inspection, wireless networking, precision agriculture, and environmental monitoring.

Before UW’s Iyer Lab I worked in Shyam Gollakota’s Networks & Mobile Systems Lab and Jinkyu Yang’s Laboratory for Engineered Materials & Structures, and spent time at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center and Whirlpool Corporation. I’ve mentored 24 Ph.D. students, 15 master’s students, and 20+ undergraduate researchers, and served as Lead TA for UW’s embedded systems and ubiquitous computing courses.
